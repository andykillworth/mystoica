Three breaches filed this week, each one the width of a single degree, a single dollar, a single letter — and none of them wider than that.

Salisbury, North Carolina

The Salisbury Post's blotter for Nov. 13, 2026 records that a larceny from a motor vehicle occurred in the 400 block of Gold Hill Drive between 9 p.m. on Nov. 10 and noon on Nov. 11, with a total estimated loss of $60, and that a separate burglary occurred in the 3000 block of West Innes Street between 2:30 and 3 a.m. on Nov. 8.

Exhibit B — Filed
Chelsea, Massachusetts

Kayem Foods Inc. of Chelsea, Mass. recalled approximately 1,584,361 pounds of ready-to-eat meat and poultry products because they may have experienced temperature abuse during storage, a problem FSIS and the firm traced to a retail customer complaint about warm product temperatures at point of delivery, which led investigators to a warehouse cooler that had experienced temperature deviations.

Symbolic Notes
A — The Stolen Interval
A theft bounded precisely in time, nine at night to noon the next day, fifteen hours of custody surrendered for sixty dollars. The small sum is the point: the breach is measured not in value but in duration.
B — The Failed Cold Chain
A million and a half pounds of meat crossed from safe to suspect not through contamination but through temperature — the boundary between preserved and spoiled is not a wall but a number on a dial.
C — The Doubled Name
Two women, two marks, one vowel apart — Larisa and Larissa — contest which spelling gets to exist in commerce, as if a name could be recalled the way a cooler's contents can.

Each exhibit is a boundary violated by the smallest possible increment — an hour window, a degree of warmth, a single letter — proving that the margin of error and the margin of identity are the same margin.

A sequence with no repetition is not silence; it is a ladder. Read in order — 6, 1, 8, 7 — the digits refuse to resolve into a single note, insisting instead that today's breach be read as a procession, not a chord.

Working Theory — The Adjacency Clause

The Adjacency Clause holds that no record filed within the same calendar week is unrelated, only unconfirmed — proximity in the docket is itself a form of custody, and custody, once established, cannot be un-filed. A vehicle loses sixty dollars' worth of contents in a window of hours; a warehouse loses its grip on temperature in a window of days; a name loses its exclusivity in a window of decades compressed into a single hearing date. Three different clocks, one shared mechanism: the interval is where the theft occurs, not the object.

The Cascade

Trace the custody chain forward, degree by degree, and the pattern completes itself by Friday.

1

Salisbury, North Carolina — At 9 p.m. on Nov. 10, an object in a parked vehicle on Gold Hill Drive passes out of its owner's custody; the gap will not be noticed until noon the next day.

2

Chelsea, Massachusetts — Somewhere in that same week, a warehouse cooler holding over a million and a half pounds of meat drifts out of its temperature range — custody of cold itself lapses, unnoticed until a customer complains of warmth at the door.

3

Alexandria, Virginia — On Nov. 12 at 1 PM, the Board convenes to decide which of two nearly identical names — Larisa, Larissa — holds rightful custody of a single vowel sound in commerce.

4

Salisbury, North Carolina — Back in Rowan County, a second breach surfaces: a structure on West Innes Street loses its own boundary between 2:30 and 3 a.m. on Nov. 8, custody of a building instead of a car.

5

By Friday the 13th, three unrelated ledgers — property, temperature, and trademark — converge on the same conclusion: every custody chain has exactly one weak link, and this week all three snapped on schedule.
