Symbolic Notes
A — The Empty Holster
A gun taken from a house does not erase the house's history of holding it; it only converts that history into an absence someone else now carries. The theft is never about the object — it is about the shape left behind.
B — The Borrowed Seal
An establishment number with no grant of inspection is a mask worn by a product that wants to pass as something it is not. Twenty-four thousand pounds of meat moved under a stamp that was never earned.
C — The Burnt Foil
What is denied still leaves a residue. The foil holds the record of combustion even after the man who carried it insists otherwise — proof outlives denial.

Each exhibit documents the same structural event seen from a different angle: something real departs, something false arrives in its place, and a physical trace is left behind to testify to the swap.

Working Theory — The Adjacency Clause

This is the Adjacency Clause again, holding as it always has: items filed within forty-eight hours of one another in unrelated jurisdictions are never coincidental, only unlabeled. The handgun's disappearance in Kentucky created a debt in the record — a verified object subtracted from the world without a corresponding entry. Boston's false-sealed chicken is that debt's counterfeit repayment, a product entering circulation under a number that was never granted, as if something had to arrive to balance what had gone missing. The chain does not close cleanly, though. It requires residue, and residue is what Alaska supplied: a burnt piece of foil, denied ownership, carrying forward the proof that something was used and consumed along the way. The gun left. The counterfeit arrived. The foil remembers both.
