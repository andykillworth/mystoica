Symbolic Notes
A — The Uninspected Crossing
Thirty thousand pounds of beef crossed a national border and sat in commerce for three months before anyone asked whether it had ever truly been let in. It is the symbol of the thing that arrives without a stamp, wholesome only by assumption.
B — The Unlogged Hour
A body reported not at a moment but across a gap — 1 p.m. to 5:36 p.m., four hours and thirty-six minutes with no fixed point inside them. It is the symbol of the interval nobody can account for, the hole punched in an otherwise ordinary afternoon.
C — The Jiggled Door
A truck in a driveway, a bell pressed, a knob turned by a hand testing whether the seal would hold. It is the symbol of the threshold-tester, the one who checks every entrance before deciding whether to claim it.

Each exhibit is a boundary that failed to certify itself in real time — a border, a clock, a door — and each was only noticed after the fact, by someone filing a report.

Working Theory — The Adjacency Clause

Under the standing Adjacency Clause, any two records separated by fewer than ten days and sharing an unresolved boundary — a border, a time gap, a door — are treated as the same event wearing different jurisdictions. The clause does not require intent. It requires only that something crossed a line without anyone present to certify the crossing, and that the crossing was later logged by a party who arrived after the fact. Aventura's beef, Salisbury's silent hours, and Leelanau's jiggled knob are three filings of the identical incident: an entry attempted, and only partially witnessed.

The Cascade

The custody trace runs west to east, then doubles back through the same failure, five times over.

1

Aventura, Florida — Beef produced May 15–20 enters U.S. commerce without import reinspection; its wholesomeness becomes, by definition, unknown the moment it clears the dock.

2

Aventura, Florida — The product sits under the shipping mark 26644-AA for nearly three months, uncut, unrepackaged, waiting for a custody record that never arrives until FSIS finds it in a routine sweep.

3

Salisbury, North Carolina — On Henderlite Street, a body is reported inside a window with no fixed hour — 1 p.m. to 5:36 p.m. — the same kind of undocumented interval, now applied to a residential block instead of a shipping crate.

4

Leelanau County, Michigan — A silver Tundra pulls into a driveway at 1:02 p.m., a bell is pressed, a knob is jiggled — a live rehearsal of the same test the beef passed silently: can this boundary be crossed without anyone noticing until later?

5

The three files close within the same reporting cycle, each one a different jurisdiction's way of admitting that a threshold went unguarded, and that the guarding only began once the crossing was already complete.

The border, the clock, and the door were never separate cases — they were the same unattended entrance, filed three times under three different names.
