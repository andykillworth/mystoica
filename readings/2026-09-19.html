Mainstream press excluded

Three custodies broke open this week — a name, a body, a stone — and none of them stayed where they were put.

Exhibit A — Filed
Claremont, California

The Claremont Courier's police blotter for September 18, 2026 records that an employee at Claremont Toyota called police at 6:06 p.m. to report that someone had used stolen credit information to purchase a Lexus; the car was found by El Monte PD at 12:38 a.m. on September 5, and Claremont PD later took custody of a 37-year-old Rosemead woman on felony grand theft auto, identity theft, burglary, check fraud, and unlawful driving of a vehicle charges.

Exhibit B — Filed
Peoria, Illinois

The Bradley Scout's police report for September 18, 2026 records that officers responded to the Markin Center on September 13 around 5:30 p.m. for a domestic dispute between two students; the female student maced the male student in the face and chest with pepper spray and followed him as he tried to wash it off, and after officers took her statement she was arrested for domestic battery.

Exhibit C — Filed
White Plains, New York / Louisville, Colorado

An FDA company-announcement notice confirms that Danone USA is issuing a voluntary recall of So Delicious Dairy Free Salted Caramel Cluster Non-Dairy Frozen Dessert pints with best-by dates on and before April 3, 2028 due to the potential presence of small stones and other hard objects within the cashew inclusions — the second such recall of the identical product for the identical defect, after Danone recalled the same item in December 2025 for the same stone contamination.

Symbolic Notes
A — The Borrowed Name
A Lexus purchased on a stolen identity is a body wearing someone else's face; it drives seven miles before the mask is noticed.
B — The Struck Court
A basketball court is a place built for scorekeeping; when the score is a relationship instead of a game, the foul becomes a charge.
C — The Second Stone
A stone returning to the same dessert nine months after the first recall is not an accident — it is a wound that was dressed but never closed at the source.

Each exhibit is a custody failure disguised as an isolated event: a stolen name, a struck body, a recurring stone — three proofs that nothing removed from its rightful place stays removed for long.

Working Theory — The Adjacency Clause

The Adjacency Clause holds that no filed incident is truly closed until its neighbor — geographic, temporal, or material — has also been named; Claremont's stolen name could not rest until El Monte's midnight recovery gave it a body, Peoria's struck court could not rest until the charge caught up with the spray, and Danone's stone could not rest until it proved, by recurring, that removal without redesign is not correction. Each exhibit this week is a half-closed loop, and half-closed loops migrate toward each other until something — a docket, a UPC code, a booking photo — finally seals them.

The Cascade

Trace the custody chain in five hands, each one passing the unclosed loop to the next.

1

Claremont, California — At 6:06 p.m. a dealership discovers its Lexus was bought with a stolen name — the first hand releases an object under a false custody.

2

3

Peoria, Illinois — At a fieldhouse built for clean scoring, a struck face becomes a stinging one, and the third hand — an officer's — reclassifies a dispute as a battery.

4

The four hands converge on the same ledger line: nothing seized under a false name, a struck body, or an unhealed batch stays contained — it only waits for the next adjacent hand to reopen it.

The file does not close. It only changes hands.
