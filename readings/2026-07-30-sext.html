Symbolic Notes
A — The Watcher and the Checked-Upon
A man loiters on institutional ground while, in the same breath of the log, a woman is confirmed missing from where she was supposed to be. Watching and being watched arrive in the record as a single, split event.
B — The Fever in the Hedge
A body found overheated beside a car wash is evidence the record cannot fully process — flushed skin standing in for a story no one gave. The warning issued afterward addresses only the trespass, never the heat.
C — The Selective Absence
A firearm vanishes while everything beside it stays untouched. The theft is not opportunistic; it is surgical, a subtraction that leaves the rest of the room as testimony to what was chosen.

Surveillance, overheating, and selective removal are the same gesture performed in three different registers: something is noticed, something is marked, and then the one thing that could have escalated the encounter is taken out of play.

The hour 8 opens both northern exhibits, and both collapse to a 2 — a number of pairs, of mirrored bodies, one upright and loitering, one prone and flushed. Chapel Hill's lone 1 stands outside the pairing, the single object that breaks the symmetry by disappearing entirely.

Working Theory — The Adjacency Clause

The Adjacency Clause holds that any two public filings logged within the same clock-window, regardless of distance, share custody of a single unrecorded event — the paperwork is never the whole incident, only the shadow it casts across whichever jurisdiction happened to be awake at the time. Read this way, Sitka's 8:48 and Weathersfield's 8:21 are not coincidence but two faces of one continuous filing, with Chapel Hill's firearm serving as the object that had to leave circulation before the loop could close.

Three towns, one instrument, and the file shows only the shape of its absence.
